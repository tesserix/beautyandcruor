What the numbers say

All 39 reels on the account, grouped by what they are. Median plays, because a couple of outliers would otherwise drag the average.

TypeReelsMedian playsReachLikes / 1k
  • Narrative pieces reach 22× everything else. "The Track" parts 1 and 2 and "The Saturation Point" — short horror films with a story, not clips of work — took 78,589 plays at the median against an account median of 3,504.
  • Process content is her worst performer. 812 median plays. Sculpting timelapses and studio clips are what artists enjoy posting and what almost nobody watches.
  • Longer reels do much better. 40 seconds and over: 11,231 median. Under 20 seconds: 1,277. That is the opposite of the usual short-form advice, and it's consistent across three years.
  • Reach has nothing to do with her following. 2,778 followers; the best reel hit 122,255 plays — 44× that. Everything arrives through Explore and Reels, so posting for the existing audience is the wrong instinct.
  • Credit posts get the least reach of anything. The Bengal Files post drew 419 plays. Instagram will not carry her credits — that is the website's job. Worth being clear about that division.

The showreel

Almost every serious effects site opens with a reel. She doesn't have one cut — but there's about ten minutes of usable footage already on the account. This is a suggested running order for a 60–75 second cut, to sit on the website and as a fourth pinned post.

0:00–0:08 — cold openfrom The Track pt 2 Straight into the strongest finished character. No logo, no titles yet.
0:08–0:15 — the turnlion build Clay sculpt cutting to the same piece applied. The one moment that proves the pipeline.
0:15–0:35 — rangeSaturation Point, burns, age, creature Four or five characters, roughly four seconds each. Range is the argument here, not any single piece.
0:35–0:50 — on setJohnson's Baby, Bengal Files Real productions with real crews. Shifts her from artist to someone who works on scale.
0:50–1:05 — closecard Name, the three strongest titles, Sydney and Mumbai, and the website. Held long enough to read.
  • No music with a vocal. It dates the reel and fights the images.
  • Cut a 16:9 version too. The vertical one suits Instagram; producers watch on laptops.
  • Before/after belongs inside the reel, as a wipe or split screen — that is the industry convention, and it's why the website doesn't need a clever slider.
  • Check releases first. Footage showing a named actor's real face next to the character is a rights question.
05

What to post

The account currently talks to other makeup artists. The people who hire her are directors, producers and actors. Those want different things.

  • Make narrative pieces the habit, not the exception. Three in three years produced her three best results by an order of magnitude. One every six to eight weeks would change the account. They don't need a budget — The Track is two people and a camera.
  • Stop posting raw process. Fold it into a narrative piece instead: the sculpt earns its place as the first four seconds of a transformation, not as its own post.
  • Write a real first line. Most captions are hashtag strings. The Johnson's post — full crew credited by name and role — is the best-written thing on the account, and it's the format a producer recognises. Do that every time.
  • Tag the production and crew. It's how her work travels to people who hire, and how she shows she knows call-sheet etiquette.
  • Keep the trigger warnings. She already does it and it's the right instinct — it signals someone who thinks about where work is shown.
  • Length: aim 40–70 seconds. Her own numbers support it, consistently.
  • Let go of follower count. At 2,778 followers she reached 122k people. The metric that matters is whether a producer who lands on the profile makes contact — so the grid's job is to survive ten seconds of scrutiny and hand them to the website.
